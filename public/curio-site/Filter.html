Filter

Most of what reaches you is noise about AI. Here's how I sort it.

✕ fallacy of exhaustion Filter answers "I don't have time." The tool is the time.

the lane shift

What's missing is hierarchy.

Stop asking what's new.

Ask what works.

You can read, and you have. The newsletters are open in tabs, the launch posts are screenshotted, the summaries have all been skimmed. What you're missing is hierarchy, and hierarchy is two things. Timing: what deserves your attention this week and what can sit until next quarter. Fit: what's right for your work, your role, and the day you run. The same launch post is essential for one person and noise for the next.

A lot of the noise is about AI and has nothing to do with using it: the bubble column, the hack headline, the prediction that it all pops by spring. Precautions are worth taking, and careful companies take them and keep moving. Your filter can skip most of the rest. One test works well: would this change what I do on Monday?

Then there's time. You're comparing AI to every tool that came before. How long did it take to get good at Excel, Asana, Figma, 3D modeling? Years. This one bends to you. There's no syntax to memorize and no certification. Saying "I don't have time to learn AI" is like saying "I don't have time to learn to drive, I have to keep walking everywhere."

Here's a filter that holds up. Every new model gets smarter at general things, and everyone gets that upgrade on the same day. You can stop tracking it closely. What changes your results is what the AI knows about you: your files, your standards, what you decided last week. In a plain chat that resets to zero every time. So give your attention to anything that helps it know your work, and let most model news go by.

That's also why AI compounds. Most people treat it like a chat: same task tomorrow, same prompt, same effort. Save the work. A skill, a saved prompt, a short note it can read next time. What took thirty minutes today takes two minutes next week, and later it runs while you're in a meeting. Stop being the one who repeats the task.

If you lead a team, a filter is something you can give them. Every newsletter they subscribe to is someone else's filter, written for nobody in particular. Imagine if Monday morning brought one channel with the two posts from last week worth their attention, and a paragraph on why. And when someone solves a task with AI, save the recipe where the rest can find it. Teach, don't do.

Skepticism is healthy. The fastest way through it is a small real task.

There's plenty to doubt: overblown promises, bad demos, tools that don't work as advertised. You're right to question it. An argument won't settle it. A task will. Whether AI companies are priced right is for investors to argue about. Whether the tools work is something you can test this afternoon. iii · what happens to my job

AI absorbs tasks. Jobs change shape.

What people are finding once they use it every day.

the rest of it

People who use it daily report the same thing: it does half the task, sometimes 80%, and they do the rest. Their part is the checking, the choosing and the judgment. That's why the questions have changed. What does a new grad learn on, if the starter work is gone? What do you do with two weeks of work done in four hours? Nobody has settled answers yet. Asking them means you're using it.
