What holds you back from AI.

Five things people say to themselves. Each one feels like a reason. Each one is a fallacy. Tap a card to flip it.

The Fallacy of Exhaustion

“I don't have time.”

turn over↻
the reframe
The tool is the time.

You're expecting a tool that takes years to master, the way Excel, CAD, Asana and Figma did. This one doesn't. You already know how to talk. Saying "I don't have time to learn AI" is like saying "I don't have time to learn to drive, I have to keep walking everywhere." Those five-year learning curves aren't coming back.

The Fallacy of Guilt

“Is this cheating?”

turn over↻
the reframe
You set the dial.

A calculator was cheating at math, until it wasn't. Google was cheating at memory, until it wasn't. The better question is how much of this task you want to keep. Turn the dial down and AI does the work. Turn it up and it teaches you to do it. You choose, task by task. Hand off the typing. Keep the judgment.

The Fallacy of Identity

“I'm the [Excel] person.”

turn over↻
the reframe
You are more than your tool.

The Excel skill, the deck skill, the [my craft] skill was the ladder. AI takes over parts of your role and leaves the job. The making moves. Choosing what's worth making stays, along with taste, judgment and relationships. A film director doesn't run the camera, and nobody thinks the film is less theirs.

The Fallacy of Muscle Memory

“Some day I'll be good at AI.”

turn over↻
the reframe
There is no someday.

Twenty years of software trained you to think "I'll set aside a weekend and get good at this." So you picture AI as one more program to learn, the way early drivers pictured a carriage without a horse. There's no curriculum and no starting line. "I don't know where to start" is the same fallacy in a different hat. Skip how do I. Say what you wish, or what you hate.

The Fallacy of Yesterday

“I tried it 8 months ago. It got it wrong.”

turn over↻
the reframe
That snapshot is old.

One bias in three shapes: "I tried it months ago," "it's a fad, the new dot-com bubble," and "I have to prompt it like a child." Each one judges today by an old snapshot. The model you tried has been replaced, probably more than once. The chat that answered one question at a time now reads your files and works on a task for hours. Run the same test again this week.

why curio · why now · the bubble

Why this exists.

Why Curio. Information was never the problem. There's a tutorial for everything, and you can ask Claude itself. What's missing is translation: someone who taught for fifteen years, brought a forty-person team along, and can put this in terms of your work.

Why Claude. It's the one I use every day, so the examples say Claude. Almost everything here works the same in ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini. The one exception is the skill file in the starter pack, which is made for Claude.

Why now. Because people are using it, and use raises new questions. If AI does half the task, or 80% of it, what's my part? What does a recent grad do first? What do I do with two weeks of work finished in four hours? The tiredness has changed too. A day of judgment calls wears you out in a way a day of typing never did. All of that is normal. It's what the change feels like from the inside.

About the bubble. The news cycle wants a pop, and it keeps reaching for 1999. Whether AI stocks are priced right is a question for investors. Whether the tools work is one you can answer at your desk this afternoon. Careful firms are taking precautions and still moving fast. Even 1999 makes the point: the stocks fell and the internet kept going. The genie is out, and it isn't going back in.

Where it's going. Every new model makes AI smarter for everyone at once. What it knows about you, your files and how you think only grows if you build it. That second part is where the advantage is, and it's what the four focuses work on.
