Who's behind this.

Curio is a free guide, written by one person who taught for fifteen years and then spent two helping people use AI. I'm Ryan.

I've spent most of my career as a teacher in disguise. Creative director, design lead, VP of Creative: the titles changed, and the job stayed the same. Take something I can see clearly and make it tangible enough that other people see it too. Fifteen years of standing in front of rooms, walking people from "I don't get it" to "oh, now I get it."

Then AI arrived, and I watched the same thing happen everywhere I looked. Smart, capable, senior people I respect froze in front of a tool that could do more for them than anything they'd been handed. They were capable. Nobody had translated it into their work, in their language.

So I started translating. I brought Claude into a forty-person agency as the way the work got done, well past a pilot. 75% of the team was using it daily within four weeks. I connected it to the tools we lived in (Excel, Asana, our financial system), and it raised roughly $433,000 in cost-recovery questions nobody had thought to ask. The tool had been available the whole time. Somebody had to aim it.

That's the experience Curio is built on: the unglamorous work of getting real people, including skeptics, the overwhelmed, and the ones who tried it once and quit, to pick the thing up and use it for something that mattered to them.

It stops being a tool.

Most people treat AI the way they treated every tool before it. Google was for search. Excel was for numbers. You learn the buttons and do the same job with less friction. That view is right as far as it goes, and it's early.

What I've watched, first in my own use and then across a whole agency, is a slow rotation. The questions start as how do I. They become what should I. Eventually they become what am I, and that one has nothing to do with software. A friend told Claude where he'd been for fifteen years and asked where his career was going. What he got back was a reading.

There's an old line, much older than Disney: mirror, mirror on the wall. You stand in front of a surface that knows more than you do, ask a question you can't answer alone, and the story turns on what you do next. I think we're building magic mirrors. The interface stays the same and the posture changes. People come to it for advice, for a second opinion on their own life, for a map.

What's changed since I started.

When I began this, the question I heard most was should I bother? I rarely hear it now. People are using AI at work, and their questions come from use. If it does half the task, what's my part? What does someone just out of school learn on? Why am I this tired after a day when so much got done?

Those are good questions, and they're signs of progress. The work has moved toward judgment, and judgment is tiring. Nobody is putting this back in the bottle, whatever the bubble headlines say. So the guide has shifted with the questions: less about whether to start, more about what to do once it's part of your day.

Focuses, fallacies, and wishes.

Curio has three parts.

Focuses are what you're looking for when you show up. There are four: Inspiration, Filter, Mindset, Solutions. They're the shapes a person's want takes when they sit down and try to say why they came. What surprised me is how rarely the focus is Solutions. Most people don't arrive with a task. They arrive with a feeling that something is bigger than they are, and they'd like a map.

Fallacies are what stop them. I started with four and I'm up to five. They're false beliefs that look like reasons and work as defenses. Exhaustion ("I don't have time"). Guilt ("is this cheating?"). Identity ("I'm the Excel person"). Muscle memory ("some day I'll be good at AI"). Yesterday ("I tried it eight months ago"). None of them is technical, and a better prompt won't fix any of them. They're the kind of block a good coach works on. The way through opens once you can name your fallacy.

Wishes are what you came for. Watch how people use AI and the requests split in two. On one side, hate: I hate my taxes, my inbox, my status reports. On the other, wish: I wish I could see how my mind works, where my company is losing money, what I'm avoiding. The hate request makes a bad thing go away and gives you time back. The wish request makes something possible that wasn't, and gives you capacity you didn't know you had. The mirror works on wish.

What I learned training people on AI.

A few things I didn't expect, and they held every time.

It's rarely a skill problem. The people who struggle are as capable as anyone. They're more defended, usually because they're the most senior and have the most to protect. A tutorial doesn't help much. Permission does.

The first real win changes everything. Demos and keynotes don't do it. What does it is the moment someone hands AI a thing they dreaded and gets it back in two minutes. After that they keep going on their own.

It compounds if you save it. The people who pull ahead use AI about as much as everyone else. They save what they figure out, so a one-time solution becomes a repeatable one. Treat each task as a deposit.

Curio is everything I'd tell you if we sat down for an hour. The quiz finds your fallacy. The focuses are the ways through. The starter pack is your first wish, made concrete. There's nothing for sale here. It's what I wish someone had handed me when I started.

It's an old practice in new clothes: stand in front of something that knows more than you do, ask a question you can't answer alone, and listen for what comes back. Skip the how. Ask it what you wish.
