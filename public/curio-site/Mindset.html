Mindset

You haven't been doing it wrong. You've been using the smartest colleague you have like a calculator.

✕ fallacy of muscle memory Mindset answers "some day I'll be good at AI." There is no someday.

✕ fallacy of yesterday And "I tried it months ago and it got things wrong." It kept improving while you were away.

the lane shift

The plateau is a question problem.

Stop treating this like software.

Say what you wish. Or what you hate.

You learned the prompts. You save time on email. You built a few good habits. Then nothing: same job, same outputs, same ceiling. You've been using a colleague like a calculator. You ask it to spell-check when you could ask it to rethink the job.

There's a path here, and most people stop on the first step. You ask: one question, one answer. You brief: you give it the background a new hire would need. You connect: it reads your files, your notes, your calendar. You train: your corrections become standing rules. You delegate: it runs whole pieces of work and brings them back for review. The plateau is what the first step feels like after a year.

What moves you along that path is context. The model arrives smart about the world and knows nothing about you. In a plain chat it forgets you every time. Everything you tell it about your work, your standards and your taste is the part no one else has. Point it at the pantry and ask for a soufflé. Don't worry how it gets there. (Check the data and check the permissions. That's the one caveat.)

Your job changes as you go. You spend less time making and more time choosing: which version, which detail, what's worth building at all. A film director doesn't run the camera, and the film is still theirs. People feel that shift as a plateau because they're still trying to run the camera.

It's tiring in a new way, and that surprises people. When two weeks of work lands in four hours, what's left for you is judgment, all day. Your brain is doing harder work for more of the day. That's normal, and it means the thing is working. Pace yourself the way you would in any job that's mostly decisions.

If you lead a team, the plateau is usually a permission problem. Half the team is fluent and half is waiting to be told they're allowed to want bigger things. Ask them two questions: what do you wish for? and what do you hate? Then do it yourself, out loud, in the real work. Teams copy what they see.

Tomorrow morning, open Claude and skip "help me write this email." Say: "I wish I had a tool that did X. Build it." Then tell it what to fix.

i · the brilliant partner

Think of it as a brilliant partner.

One who has read widely, never sleeps, and doesn't take it personally when you say "that's not quite right."

A search engine is the wrong comparison. It's closer to a coworker who has read more briefs, strategy docs and textbooks than any person could, and can apply them to your situation while you watch. You still direct it. The more you tell it about your work, your clients and your standards, the more it acts like a member of the team. And it's available at 11pm.

ii · the blank page

What if you never started from a blank page again?

The hardest moment in creative work is the blank page.

the rest of it

Ask for ten directions on a brief. They won't all be good, and some will be obvious. Two or three will spark something you wouldn't have found alone. You're still the one with the taste. It makes sure you always have something to react to, and reacting is easier than inventing.

Try this: ask for ten directions, then tell it which two are close. turn back↺
iii · two kinds of smart

It knows the world. It doesn't know you.

You can start with no setup at all. Getting past the plateau takes context.

turn over↻
the rest of it

There are two kinds of intelligence here. The general kind comes in the box and improves with every release, for everyone at once. The second kind is what it knows about you, and it starts at zero. Nobody can sell you that part. You build it by connecting your files, saving what works, and correcting it when it's off. A smart stranger gives general answers. A colleague who knows your work gives yours.

Try this: start one file called "about my work" and add to it every week. turn back↺
try it

Make a wish.

Each of these starts as a wish, or as something you hate. Skip the plan and the project brief. Say it, and see what's on your desk tomorrow.

I your own memory

A memory
that answers

Imagine if you could ask what idea you had eleven months ago about a campaign, and get it back. Start a folder of notes and give it access.

II the hated task

The thing
you hate

Imagine if the report you dread every Friday wrote its own first draft. Describe the hate in detail. Don't polish the prompt.

III the missing tool

The tool
you wish existed

Imagine if the small tool you keep mocking up in your head existed by lunch. Say: I wish I had a tool that did X. Build it.

IV the standing rule

The correction
you keep making

Imagine if you never gave the same note twice. The next time you fix its tone or its format, ask it to save that as a rule.
